# AirGuard: A credible social-impact pilot

AirGuard has a meaningful social aim, but software launches and optimistic claims are not impact evidence. Use this small, ethical, repeatable pilot to learn whether the product helps people make decisions.

## 1. Question
Can a city-level, uncertainty-labeled air-quality decision tool help a student, caregiver or outdoor worker make a more informed plan compared with a conventional AQI page?

## 2. Pilot design
- Ask one willing school, parent group or community organization for permission. Do not contact children directly without parental/school consent.
- Recruit 15–30 **voluntary adults**, or another feasible, clearly reported sample. Record the actual number.
- Before showing AirGuard, ask participants how they would choose the timing of an outdoor task and how confident they feel about finding trustworthy pollution data. Do not solicit personal medical histories.
- Give participants both a conventional AQI site and AirGuard for the **same city and forecast date**, in randomized order if possible.
- Ask them to find a lower-PM2.5 one-hour window and create a credible communication note.
- Measure task completion, elapsed time, correct interpretation of "model estimate", and willingness to use the tool; permit participants to decline.
- After 7 days, ask whether any recommendations were used, but label self-reported changes as self-reported. Do not infer cleaner ambient air.

## 3. Metrics and transparent reporting

| Metric | Measurement | What it does NOT prove |
|---|---|---|
| Finding a lower-forecast-PM2.5 slot | Correct window identified in a fixed scenario | Actual inhaled dose reductions |
| Understanding uncertainty | Can explain model forecast vs sensor observation | Health literacy beyond this topic |
| Task completion time | Stopwatch/time stamp, both tools | Long-term adoption |
| Notes reviewed or sent | Voluntary participant report | Institutional policy change |
| Real policy response | Documented written response with permission | Ambient emissions reduction |
| Participant feedback | De-identified thematic summary | Representative citywide sentiment |

## 4. Data protection and fairness
- Only collect informed-consent, de-identified feedback.
- Avoid names, precise home/school locations, phone numbers and medical records.
- Translate questions into Urdu for participants who prefer it; let them correct your translations.
- Never imply that a lower modeled concentration is necessarily below a health threshold.
- Allow participants to report when the recommendation could not be used because of job or school schedules. This informs equitable design.

## 5. Publish what did *not* work
Track failed forecast loads, number of unanswerable questions, misinterpretation of forecast source, barriers to action, and any recommendations considered impractical. Include the number of test participants, dates and methodology in any college or competition submission.

## 6. Scientific next step
Collect independent official or reference-monitor observations at matching times and locations. Evaluate bias and error of the underlying modeled PM2.5 series, including seasonal changes, before claiming accurate forecasting or health benefit. Only add individual risk or local routes after appropriate validation and geospatial data.
